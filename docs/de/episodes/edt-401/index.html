Episode 401 How to Build a Resilient Team Without Toxic Positivity

2026-11-12 Hosted by Dr. Darren Pulsipher

Explore more in the episode archive.

Summary

Russell Harvey makes a clear case that resilience is not about telling people to toughen up or stay positive. It is about helping individuals and teams move forward with learning after pressure, loss, or disruption. For executives, the real issue is not whether people can endure change, but whether the organization creates the conditions for people to adapt, perform, and keep moving. That means removing blockers, role-modeling healthy leadership, and replacing toxic positivity with grounded optimism. The conversation is especially relevant for leaders navigating constant change, because it reframes resilience as something that can be designed, taught, and reinforced.

Resilience Is a Leadership Practice, Not a Slogan

AI may be changing how organizations work, but the deeper challenge is still human: how people respond to pressure, uncertainty, and change. That is why resilience matters so much to leaders. It is not just about coping when things go wrong. It is about building the habits, mindset, and culture that help people and organizations keep moving forward with clarity and purpose.

Russell, a leadership and resilience coach, makes a strong case that resilience is not a trait you simply have or do not have. It is something that can be developed, practiced, and modeled. For executives, that shifts the conversation from “How do we survive disruption?” to “How do we create the conditions where people can learn, adapt, and perform?”

Why Resilience Is Under Pressure

Russell argues that resilience has been strained by a few major forces: social media, the pandemic, and now AI. These systems can reduce the chances for people to build resilience in ordinary life. Instead of dealing with discomfort directly, people may look to technology for an answer before they look to other humans or take action themselves.

He gave a simple but telling example: someone who feels lonely may ask an AI chatbot what to do, rather than step outside and connect with people. That pattern worries him because resilience usually grows through real-world friction, not avoidance. If every difficult feeling is handed off to a tool, people may lose the chance to build the emotional muscle they need.

This concern is not just personal. It affects organizations too. When leaders and teams become overly dependent on quick answers, they can become less willing to sit with uncertainty, challenge assumptions, or face hard truths. In Russell’s view, that is where resilience starts to erode.

From Bounce Back to Spring Forward

Russell is not a fan of the common phrase “bounce back.” To him, it suggests returning to exactly where you were before, as if nothing changed. His definition is different: resilience is “springing forward with learning.” In other words, difficult experiences should leave you wiser, not unchanged.

That definition matters because it changes what leaders should ask after a setback. Instead of asking, “How do we get back to normal?” the better question is, “What did we learn, what should we keep doing, and what should we do differently?” That is a more practical and more honest definition of growth.

He also warned against “toxic resilience” and “toxic positivity.” These show up when leaders brush off legitimate concern with phrases like “just be positive” or “just be resilient.” That does not build strength; it shuts down conversation. Russell’s alternative is optimism grounded in reality. Honest optimism means acknowledging the size of the challenge while also recognizing the team’s strengths, capabilities, and options.

What Leaders, Parents, and Teachers Can Do

Russell’s framework for resilient leadership is refreshingly concrete. He says leaders are responsible for three things: delegate brilliantly to people’s strengths and remove blockers, intentionally build a resilient team, and work on their own personal resilience. Most leaders, he noted, spend far too little time on those priorities.

That same thinking applies outside the workplace. For parents and educators, resilience is not built by shielding people from every failure. It is built by helping them process disappointment, recover, and learn. Russell’s broader resilience wheel includes seven areas: attitude, purpose, adaptability, confidence, support network, meaning, and energy. These are not abstract ideas; they are practical areas people can assess and strengthen.

One insight that stands out is his view of confidence. He pushes back on the term “imposter syndrome,” arguing that confidence is not binary and should not be treated as a medical label. People have different ways of building confidence, and the task is to learn which methods work for each person. That is a useful reminder for any leader trying to develop talent: resilience grows when people understand themselves better.

The conversation ultimately lands on a simple but powerful idea. Resilience is not about pretending things are fine. It is about creating the conditions for honesty, learning, and forward movement. If that matters in your organization, this full conversation is well worth your time.
