Episode 398 How to Find Invisible Labor Before You Automate

2026-11-04 Hosted by Dr. Darren Pulsipher

Explore more in the episode archive.

Summary

Margot Lawless argues that invisible labor is one of the most overlooked sources of organizational friction. It includes the coordination, follow-up, rework, and tribal knowledge that keep a business functioning but rarely appear in formal plans or headcount assumptions. For executives, the issue is not just employee burnout; it is growth capacity. When hidden work accumulates, leaders feel it as slower scaling, overloaded managers, and high performers leaving. The larger lesson is that AI will not fix a system leaders do not understand. If organizations automate only the visible tasks, they risk adding more work instead of reducing it. The real opportunity is to identify invisible labor, locate its sources, and redesign the system around clearer ownership, better coordination, and smarter use of talent.

The Hidden Work That Shapes Team Performance and AI Success

Invisible labor is one of those ideas that executive teams can feel before they can name it. It shows up as the person everyone goes to for answers, the meetings that end without action items, the process that somehow works only because one employee quietly keeps it together. For technology and business leaders trying to improve productivity, adopt AI, or scale an organization, this hidden work matters more than most realize.

What makes the concept powerful is that it is not just about overwork. It is about work that is necessary, valuable, and often unassigned. When leaders cannot see it, they cannot manage it well. When AI is introduced without understanding it, the hidden work does not disappear — it often gets worse.

Invisible labor is the work that keeps the organization running

Margot describes invisible labor as the unassigned work that holds systems together. It is not simply tribal knowledge, though that is one example. It includes the informal coordination, follow-up, rework, and behind-the-scenes problem solving that make a team function when the formal process is incomplete or unclear.

A useful example came from a simple birthday party. One parent does not just “take a child to a party.” They RSVP, buy the gift, wrap it, locate the waiver, and manage a dozen small tasks that are easy to overlook. In the workplace, the same pattern appears when one person quietly bridges gaps in process, communication, or ownership.

Margot calls these people the “organizational duct tape” or “spackle” of a company. They know how to fix things, connect people, and keep momentum going. The challenge is that this work is often invisible to senior leaders, even though the organization depends on it every day.

The real problem is not the symptom — it is the source

One of the most practical ideas in the conversation is that invisible labor creates symptoms, but the symptoms are not the root cause. A team may have too many meetings, chronic delays, or burned-out high performers. Simply adding a “no-meeting Friday” or another tactical fix will not solve the deeper issue.

Margot says the sources of invisible labor usually fall into six categories: clarity, coordination, ownership, systems, accountability, and incentives. When those are weak or misaligned, people create work behind the scenes to compensate. That compensation may keep the company running, but it also hides the real structural problem.

This is where executive visibility matters. If growth starts to feel heavier, if managers are pulled into firefighting instead of leadership, or if top performers begin leaving, those are signs that invisible labor may be accumulating. In other words, the company may be paying a hidden tax on its own inefficiency.

AI can reduce invisible labor — or amplify it

The conversation around AI often assumes the system is already well understood. Margot pushes back on that assumption. AI can absolutely reduce invisible labor, but only if leaders first make the invisible work visible. Otherwise, automation may only address the documented process while leaving the real work untouched.

That can create a false sense of progress. A document that used to take 20 minutes to draft might now take 2 minutes to generate — but then still take 20 or 30 minutes to edit, correct, and align with the real need. In that case, AI has not removed work; it has shifted it, and possibly added more.

There is also a human side to this. High performers often absorb invisible labor because they are good at it, they enjoy being helpful, and it reinforces their sense of value. But over time, that behavior can hide system weaknesses and create dependency. Leaders need to see both sides: the organizational design issues that cause invisible labor, and the personal incentives that keep it in place.

The takeaway is simple but powerful: if you want AI to help your organization, start by understanding the hidden work already there. Listen to the full conversation to hear how Margot explains the pattern, the pressure, and what leaders can do about it.
