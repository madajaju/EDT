Episode 399 How to Build an Organizational Digital Twin

2026-11-05 Hosted by Dr. Darren Pulsipher

Explore more in the episode archive.

Summary

This episode makes a strong case that digital transformation needs a better operating model, not just better tooling. Michael Schank explains why enterprises should model the organization itself: its structure, processes, controls, roles, and performance signals. That perspective matters because the biggest transformation risks often come from hidden work, inconsistent language, and incomplete knowledge. The practical takeaway is clear: start with a measurable business outcome, build the semantic layer around it, and keep the model alive as the business changes.

Why a Digital Twin of the Organization Could Change Enterprise Transformation

Digital twins have already proven their value in factories and physical systems, where sensors and models help leaders see what is happening in real time and predict what comes next. The next frontier is more abstract but arguably more important: the organization itself. For CIOs, CTOs, architects, and business leaders, that shift matters because many of the hardest transformation problems are not technical alone. They are about how people, processes, systems, data, and governance all fit together.

Michael Schank makes a practical case for treating the enterprise as a system that can be modeled, analyzed, and improved. His perspective is useful because it moves digital transformation away from buzzwords and toward something executives can actually use to reduce risk, accelerate change, and make better decisions.

From technical delivery to systems thinking

Schank’s path into systems thinking started in classic technology roles. He began his career as a Java and C programmer, moved into solution architecture, and led large system integration efforts. At that stage, his focus was narrow and familiar to many technical leaders: deliver the code, keep defects down, and make sure production stayed stable.

That changed when his work pulled him closer to the business side. At EY, he was asked to build a business capability model for a retail bank. That experience gave him a new lens. Instead of thinking only in terms of applications and code, he began to see how business capabilities could connect strategy, operations, technology, data, and risk. Later, on a mortgage company engagement, he was exposed to process modeling at a broader scale and saw how important it was to understand what people actually do on the ground.

What emerged from those experiences was a systems view of transformation. Rather than optimizing a single process in isolation, Schank argues for understanding the whole landscape of work. That is a more demanding approach, but it is often the one that prevents expensive mistakes.

Why an enterprise digital twin is different

A common reaction to the idea of a digital twin of an organization is: isn’t everything already digital? The answer is no. Enterprise systems such as ERP platforms may hold finance or supply chain data, but they rarely capture the full set of informal handoffs, cross-team dependencies, and undocumented work that keep an organization running.

Schank calls this the “AI context gap.” Large language models can know a lot about the world, but they do not automatically know how a specific company operates. A digital twin fills that gap by creating a semantic layer: a structured model of organizational units, processes, systems, roles, products, risks, and controls. Once that layer exists, AI can do more than generate generic answers. It can help scope a transformation, estimate impact, draft requirements, and identify likely dependencies.

That matters in large, complex efforts such as ERP implementations. Schank points to the risk of expensive failures when organizations transform core finance and supply chain systems without a clear model of current-state operations. His argument is straightforward: if you can map the organization before the project starts, you can ask better questions, make better tradeoffs, and avoid costly surprises.

How leaders should think about adoption and ROI

Schank is clear that this is not a “build it once and forget it” exercise. Organizations change. Reorgs happen. New projects launch. Policies shift. A useful digital twin has to be maintained by a team that validates the model on a regular cycle and updates it when change is released into production.

That maintenance burden is real, so the business case has to be equally real. Schank recommends starting with the “why” and tying the effort to measurable outcomes. Those outcomes might include productivity, faster change delivery, lower risk, or better compliance. Without a clear metric, leaders can end up in endless architecture debates about capabilities, processes, or frameworks without ever proving value.

The most compelling use cases are the ones that connect the model to decisions. For risk and compliance teams, that might mean automated assessments and better audit planning. For transformation teams, it could mean better scoping, stronger requirements, and more accurate test plans. Schank also sees value in using live metrics to understand not only how the organization works, but how well it is performing. That opens the door to questions executives care about: where is waste hiding, where is risk accumulating, and what will happen if conditions change?

A digital twin of the organization is not a magic answer. But for enterprises trying to transform faster with less risk, it may become one of the most practical tools available. Listen to the full conversation to hear how Schank is turning that idea into a working platform.
