Episode 395 A Practical Way to Research Ballot Propositions with AI

2026-10-22 Hosted by Dr. Darren Pulsipher

Explore more in the episode archive.

Summary

This episode is about decision quality under information overload. Evan Meyer argues that voters do not need more outrage; they need a process. By defining priorities, using AI to research propositions, and checking claims against source material, individuals can make decisions that are more consistent and less reactive. For executives, the lesson is broader: any high-stakes environment benefits from a repeatable framework that turns complexity into judgment.

Evan Meyer

How to Become a Better Informed Voter in the Age of Misinformation

Voters today are flooded with content designed to provoke, not inform. Robocalls, campaign ads, social posts, texts, and mailers all compete for attention, but much of it is optimized for outrage. The result is predictable: people feel exhausted, cynical, and unsure how to evaluate the issues that will shape their neighborhoods, states, and country.

That matters because civic decisions are not abstract. Local policy affects trash pickup, lighting, schools, zoning, public safety, and the quality of life in a community. The conversation around informed voting is really a conversation about how to replace reaction with judgment—and how modern tools, including AI, can help.

Why local engagement still matters

One of the strongest themes in the discussion was that people often underestimate the power of local action. It is easy to assume that politics only happens in Washington or at the state capitol, but most of the issues that affect daily life are decided much closer to home. City councils, neighborhood associations, chambers of commerce, business improvement districts, and school boards all influence how communities function.

That is where change often starts. If something works in one community, it can be scaled elsewhere. The same logic applies in business: prove value in one setting, then expand. Local government is no different. When citizens show up, ask questions, and stay engaged, they can influence outcomes that are tangible and immediate.

The challenge is that many people have become disengaged because they feel their input does not matter. Social media has only made that worse. Posting anger online can feel like participation, but it usually deepens polarization rather than improving policy. In the words of the conversation, rage is not civic engagement.

A practical way to research your ballot

The first step to better voting is accepting that it takes time. No one can become fully informed in a few minutes, and most voters are forced to choose between imperfect options. That does not mean disengaging. It means approaching the process with humility and realism.

A useful method is to define your own priorities before you look at candidates or propositions. Instead of trying to care about everything, identify your top five values. That might be public safety, education, climate, transportation, economics, or reproductive rights. Rank them. Then use those priorities as the filter for everything else.

That framework is especially helpful when evaluating ballot propositions. These measures are often written in dense legal language and wrapped in emotionally charged campaigns. A proposition may sound like one thing in an ad while containing consequences buried deep in the text. Reviewing the official ballot language is a start, but it is not enough by itself.

This is where AI can help. A tool like Claude can be used as a research assistant: feed it your values, ask for an impartial summary of the proposition, and request a plain-English explanation of the likely tradeoffs. You can also ask it to compare the official language with independent analysis, or even prompt it to examine possible second- and third-order effects. The key is to use it as a guide, not a replacement for judgment.

Using AI without outsourcing your judgment

The conversation made a strong case that AI can reduce some of the friction in civic research. It can help voters process long documents, compare claims, and stay consistent across multiple decisions. It can even generate a one-page voter guide based on your own priorities so you can share your reasoning with friends without telling them how to vote.

But AI is not neutral in a perfect sense. Like any system trained on data, it can reflect bias. That means voters should cross-check outputs, ask where a claim came from, and compare answers across multiple tools when something seems off. The point is not to trust AI blindly. It is to use it as one input among several.

That discipline matters because emotional voting can have real consequences. The conversation pointed to examples where a well-intended emotional response led to long-term policy problems. Better voting means thinking beyond the ad, beyond the headline, and beyond the loudest voice in your feed. It means choosing based on values, evidence, and the likely outcomes of a decision—not on outrage alone.

The broader message was simple: democratic participation is worth the effort. It is imperfect, but it is still the best system we have. If voters take the time to understand what they are deciding, they can make more responsible choices for themselves and their communities.
