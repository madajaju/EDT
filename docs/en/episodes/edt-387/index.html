Episode 387 Why Technical Leaders Need Storytelling Before Slides

2026-09-22 Hosted by Dr. Darren Pulsipher

Explore more in the episode archive.

Summary

Clarity wins in the age of AI. Host Dr. Darren sits down with Nir Zavaro to unpack how leaders, engineers, and founders can turn complex ideas into clear, memorable stories—without hiding behind slides. From AI-assisted drafting to presentation structure and emotional storytelling, this conversation is a practical guide to better business communication, public speaking, and storytelling for leaders.

Key Takeaways

  • Slides should support the message, not carry it. If the deck is doing all the work, the story needs more strength.
  • Start with the outcome. Before building a talk, decide what the audience should think, feel, or do afterward.
  • Emotion gives logic traction. Facts matter, but people act when the message connects emotionally.
  • Use AI to reduce friction, not thinking. Transcribe, draft, shorten, and refine with AI tools, but keep control of the core idea.
  • Practice makes presentations clearer. Rehearsing your opener, closer, and key scenes helps you speak with confidence and precision.
  • Focus on your audience, not your ego. The best talks are about their needs, not how impressive you are.

A better way to communicate

The biggest problem with many presentations isn’t the data — it’s the delivery. Nir argues that too many professionals, especially in tech, use slides as a crutch instead of treating them as a support tool for storytelling.

That matters for founders, executives, engineers, and anyone trying to win trust, drive action, or explain something complex. In a world where AI tools can help you draft and refine ideas, the real advantage is learning how to communicate clearly and persuasively.

Story first, slides second

Build the talk before you build the deck

Nir’s core message is simple: if your slides disappeared, would the idea still survive? If not, the talk needs work. He recommends starting with the story itself, then shaping the visuals to reinforce it.

One practical method is to outline your talk in sections: opener, body, and closer. From there, script the first 70 words carefully, because that opening has to grab attention fast and set the tone.

Key takeaways

  • Define the one thing your audience should remember.

  • Use slides to amplify your message, not carry it.

  • Write the opener and closer first.

  • Cut anything that doesn’t change the meaning.

Why emotion beats information overload

Facts inform, but feelings drive decisions

Nir makes a strong point that decisions are rarely made on facts alone. In business, people respond to clarity, confidence, and emotion — then the data helps justify the choice.

That’s especially true in technical and enterprise settings. A founder pitching investors, an engineer presenting a roadmap, or a leader announcing change all need to answer the same question: why should this matter to the audience right now?

How AI can help you speak more clearly

Use AI to reduce friction, not thinking

AI is most useful when it helps turn rough ideas into something workable. Nir suggests using it to transcribe, draft, shorten, and refine, especially if writing or organizing thoughts feels difficult.

But the goal isn’t to generate more content. It’s to make your message clearer, faster. If you can record your thoughts, turn them into a draft, and edit for relevance, you’ll improve both your speaking and your writing.

Put the audience at the center

Make the talk about them, not you

A common trap for speakers is trying to prove how smart or experienced they are. Nir flips that idea: the point is not to showcase yourself, but to help the audience understand, decide, and act.

That mindset shift changes everything. Whether you’re pitching, teaching, or leading a team, the real test is simple: can your idea stand on its own without the slides?
