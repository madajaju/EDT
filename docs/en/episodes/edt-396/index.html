Episode 396 Why AI Makes Team Conflict Harder, Not Easier

2026-10-27 Hosted by Dr. Darren Pulsipher

Explore more in the episode archive.

Summary

This episode argues that the hardest part of leading modern teams is not managing obvious differences, but surfacing the hidden ones that shape behavior under pressure. Csaba Toth makes a compelling case that culture, personality, and conditioning all interact, but that organizations often mistake stereotypes for understanding. His deeper point is that AI does not remove this problem; it can actually make it worse by giving people more ways to avoid friction, challenge, and self-examination. The executive lesson is straightforward: if you want better decisions, you need more than diversity on paper. You need structured self-awareness, facilitated disagreement, and the discipline to make comfort slightly harder than growth.

Why Cultural Intelligence Matters More in an AI-Augmented Workplace

As organizations become more global, more hybrid, and more dependent on AI, leaders are running into a deceptively simple problem: people do not think, react, or collaborate the same way. That is true across countries, across generations, and increasingly across the boundary between human work and machine assistance. The challenge for CIOs, CTOs, and business leaders is no longer just building efficient teams. It is building teams that can work well together when the default assumption is no longer shared understanding.

The Real Divide Is Not Just National Culture

Csaba Toth makes a useful distinction: culture does matter, but not always in the way people assume. He argues that the common tendency to reduce people to national labels—German, American, Hungarian, and so on—creates false confidence. You may learn the “norm” for a country, but that does not tell you how any one person thinks or behaves.

That matters because modern work is more complex than international travel or relocation. People now belong to many cultural groups at once: generation, gender, profession, education, company culture, and more. In that sense, almost every workplace conversation is cross-cultural, even when everyone is in the same city.

Toth also challenges the idea that globalization has erased these differences. Yes, the internet makes it easier to find practical answers—how to open a bank account, how to rent office space, how to communicate across time zones. But those are surface-level questions. Belonging, trust, and emotional fit still require human understanding, and those are not things you can search for.

Why Teams Break Down Under Pressure

One of Toth’s strongest points is that people reveal more of their real selves under pressure. In calm conditions, it is easy to appear aligned. Under stress, differences in values, assumptions, and communication styles become visible. That is why teams that go through demanding experiences together often bond deeply: the pressure exposes what is real.

He applies that idea directly to team performance. Cognitive diversity—the presence of different ways of thinking—is valuable, but only if it is handled well. Without structure, diversity can become confusion, ego clashes, or silent disengagement. The goal is not to eliminate disagreement. It is to make disagreement productive.

That is where facilitation matters. Toth describes a process that helps teams understand one another faster by using assessments, structured questions, and what he calls “passports” that explain how people prefer to think and work. Simple questions like “What do people most misunderstand about me?” can prevent unnecessary conflict before it starts.

AI Can Support Better Thinking, or Reinforce Bad Thinking

The conversation becomes especially timely when AI enters the picture. Toth sees the appeal of AI clearly: it is predictable, agreeable, and emotionally easy to engage with. For some people, especially those who feel isolated, AI can feel safer than human interaction because it rarely rejects, challenges, or judges.

But that safety has a cost. If AI becomes an echo chamber, it can reinforce a person’s assumptions instead of improving them. Toth’s answer is not to avoid AI, but to use it in a way that encourages cognitive flexibility—the ability to consider other perspectives and question one’s own certainty.

For teams, that means asking better questions, not just collecting better data. For individuals, it means practicing “purposeful doubt,” such as asking: What if the opposite were true? What would the evidence look like? That small shift can open the door to better decisions, especially when AI is involved and the temptation is to accept the first answer that feels right.

Toth’s broader warning is that modern life makes it too easy to avoid discomfort. We can block people, stay online, and never work through conflict. But the very skills organizations need—debate, empathy, perspective-taking, and self-awareness—only grow when people are willing to do the hard work.

If you lead people, shape culture, or are trying to make AI more useful without making your organization less human, this conversation is worth your time. Listen to the full discussion to hear the practical framework behind these ideas.
