Episode 404 How to Govern GPUs, Agents, and AI Workloads

Summary

David Williams argues that AI is not simply another workload to absorb; it is a structural change that breaks assumptions baked into decades of infrastructure design. Traditional tools were built for human-paced systems and predictable provisioning. AI agents and LLM-driven workflows consume resources differently, change more quickly, and create governance risks that many enterprises are not equipped to handle. The executive lesson is clear: security, policy, and optimization must be designed into the platform, not added later.

AI Is Changing Infrastructure Faster Than Governance Can Keep Up

The rise of generative AI is not just another technology cycle. It is pushing infrastructure teams, security leaders, and business executives into a new operating model where compute demand is less predictable, more autonomous, and harder to govern with traditional tools. What makes this moment different is that AI does not simply consume resources when a person clicks “run.” It can continue to learn, request more capacity, and trigger actions at machine speed.

That shift is forcing leaders to rethink assumptions that worked for decades. Data centers, cloud platforms, edge environments, and now AI workloads all have different economics and different control requirements. The challenge is no longer simply where to run workloads. It is how to keep them secure, efficient, and manageable when the workload itself is increasingly making decisions.

From Virtualization to AI: A Familiar Pattern, with a New Twist

David Williams has seen major infrastructure shifts before: clustering, virtualization, cloud, and edge computing. Each wave made technology easier to consume, but also introduced another layer of complexity. Virtualization pooled compute and storage into shared resources. Cloud made infrastructure feel elastic and on demand. Edge pushed intelligence closer to the source of data, whether in a store, a device, or a local site.

AI is different because it changes the relationship between users and infrastructure. Traditional software executes code written by people, usually against a known outcome. AI, especially large language models and agents, can generate code, recommend actions, and interact with systems in ways that are not fully visible to the people using them. That makes the environment harder to predict and harder to control with the old playbook.

Williams also points out that organizations rarely get to throw away the previous generation of technology. Banks, manufacturers, insurers, and most large enterprises still run a mix of on-premises systems, cloud services, virtualized platforms, and edge deployments. AI has arrived on top of all of that, not after it. That means leaders are now managing a layered estate, not a clean replacement cycle.

Why AI Makes Infrastructure Governance Harder

One of the biggest differences with AI is that it behaves like a living system rather than a fixed program. A model learns over time, consumes resources continuously, and often interacts with endpoints, people, and other agents. That means it can keep changing even after deployment. In practical terms, production is no longer a stable endpoint; it is part of the learning loop.

That creates a serious governance problem. If an AI system is making hundreds of changes a second, a human approval process cannot keep up. Williams argues that change management must become tightly connected to the systems that do the actual work. Security and policy cannot be bolted on afterward. They need to be built into the infrastructure layer itself.

This is where many organizations are exposed. Traditional tools were built to provision systems, not to optimize GPU usage, monitor AI behavior, or govern autonomous agents. GPUs, or graphics processing units, are especially important because AI workloads rely on them heavily. Yet many enterprises are underutilizing these expensive resources, even while facing shortages and supply constraints.

The Next Model Looks Federated, Not Centralized

The emerging answer, according to Williams, is not a single giant platform or a fully decentralized free-for-all. It is federation: a model where organizations can share, allocate, and monetize compute across environments. That could mean cloud, on-premises systems, edge sites, or some combination of all three. The key is being able to see capacity, control access, and direct workloads intelligently.

This is a major shift in how infrastructure should be thought about. Instead of treating the data center as an overhead cost, organizations may begin to see compute as a shared asset that can create value. That idea is not new in principle. Amazon Web Services grew out of Amazon’s own need to smooth out expensive peaks in demand. But AI raises the stakes because it is far more resource-hungry and far less predictable.

Williams also warns that agentic AI will accelerate this pressure. Agents can generate other agents, request more compute, and operate with much more autonomy than humans. That means the real challenge is not just technical scale. It is designing infrastructure that can enforce policy, optimize resources, and maintain control while machine-driven workloads expand.

The bottom line is simple: AI is exposing weaknesses in infrastructure architecture that have been ignored for years.
