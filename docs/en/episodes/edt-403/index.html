Why Quantum-Resilient Security Starts with Better Entropy

Cybersecurity is moving into a new era where traditional assumptions no longer hold. Leaders can no longer rely on the idea that systems are secure simply because they have always been secure, or because attackers have not yet had the right tools to break them. As quantum computing and AI mature, the risk is not just faster attacks — it is a fundamental change in what can be discovered, predicted, and exploited.

That shift matters for anyone responsible for protecting identity, data, and infrastructure. The conversation around post-quantum security is not just about stronger algorithms. It is about the source of trust itself, and whether the building blocks underneath encryption can withstand a new class of threats.

Complexity Is Rising, but So Is the Need for Simplicity

Andy Laurence described his long career in technology as centered on one practical superpower: making complex technology digestible. That point lands especially well in cybersecurity, where the technical depth is growing quickly, but the business need is still simple. Leaders need security that can be understood, deployed, and trusted.

That tension is visible across modern computing. Abstraction layers such as virtualization, containers, and higher-level software interfaces make systems easier to use, but they also hide more complexity beneath the surface. At the same time, AI is pushing interactions even further toward natural language. People can now ask a computer for what they want instead of navigating menus and websites. That is powerful, but it also raises a hard question: can we trust what the system returns?

The answer, today, is not automatically yes. AI can move at machine speed, well beyond human limits, and that creates a new asymmetry. Defenders and attackers alike can use it, but the attacker who uses AI to search for vulnerabilities, patterns, and weaknesses gains enormous leverage. In security, speed and scale change the game.

Why Obscurity and Classical Entropy Are No Longer Enough

One of the most important themes in the discussion was the failure of “security by obscurity.” If an organization assumes its protection will hold because the pattern is hard to see, that assumption becomes fragile in the age of AI. Machine learning is good at recognizing patterns humans miss, especially as relationships become more layered and less obvious.

Quantum computing adds another dimension to that problem. Traditional encryption depends on randomness, often called entropy, to generate keys. Entropy is the unpredictable fuel behind cryptographic strength. But if a future attacker can use quantum capability to predict or reverse engineer the source of that randomness, then the secrecy of the key itself is weakened.

That is why the entropy source matters so much. If the random seed is based on classical measurements — things like latency, power variation, or other system jitter — it may still be vulnerable to prediction. Laurence’s point was direct: the industry needs to move from classical physics-derived entropy to quantum-based entropy if it wants to stay ahead of quantum threats.

The Bigger Shift: Hardware Roots of Trust and Crypto Agility

The most practical insight from the conversation is that post-quantum security is not just about algorithms. It is also about identity, attestation, and trust at the hardware level. A hardware root of trust is a reliable foundation embedded in hardware that can help prove a system is authentic. In plain terms, it is a way to verify that something really is what it claims to be.

That matters for more than servers and devices. It also applies to workloads, virtual machines, containers, and even the systems that connect to sensitive services like online banking. If attackers can spoof identity, they can impersonate users, systems, or services. A hardware-based identity model helps establish that the connection is real and trusted.

Laurence also emphasized a key architectural principle: crypto agility. That means being able to adapt as new standards and algorithms emerge. His analogy was memorable — the algorithm is the engine, but entropy is the fuel. The best security posture is not tied to a single vendor, algorithm, or form factor. It is built to support change without redesigning the entire stack.

That is the real operational takeaway for technology leaders. The next phase of cybersecurity will require stronger algorithms, better entropy, and hardware-backed trust working together. Organizations that treat those as separate concerns will move too slowly. Those that design for agility will be better positioned for the quantum era.
