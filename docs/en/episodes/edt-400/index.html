Episode 400 How to Build an Innovation Marketplace

Summary

This episode reframes innovation as an operating system problem. Terry Young argues that organizations already produce plenty of ideas, prototypes, and research, but too many of them stall because the surrounding structures reward comfort, speed, and incrementalism. He proposes possibility sciences as a discipline for redesigning innovation itself: connecting ideas horizontally, preserving provenance, enabling reuse, and building pathways from invention to impact. For executive leaders, the message is direct: if your organization cannot move ideas across silos and into new contexts, AI will only accelerate the same limitations.

Why Innovation Gets Stuck — and How Possibility Sciences Tries to Break It Free

Organizations do not usually fail because they lack ideas. They fail because they have no good system for turning ideas into outcomes that survive budgeting cycles, legal reviews, internal politics, and the steady pull of “the way we do things here.” That tension sits at the center of Terry Young’s thinking on possibility sciences: a framework for helping leaders move beyond incremental innovation and toward real transformation.

Young argues that many companies and institutions are designed to reward comfort, not curiosity. The result is a familiar pattern: ideas are generated, discussed, narrowed, and often buried before they can create meaningful change. His message to executives is direct—if you want different results, you may need to redesign the system that produces them.

The real barrier to innovation is not ideas

Young’s core argument is that most organizations do not have an idea problem. They have a “possibility gap”: too many ideas, too few bridges to carry them into reality. In practice, that means the system often rewards small improvements to existing structures rather than bold redesign.

He traces this mindset to both education and corporate life, where people are trained to color inside the lines, specialize deeply, and stay within inherited boundaries. Competence is valuable, but Young says it can also become a trap. The more comfortable an organization becomes with its own expertise, the less likely it is to challenge the assumptions underneath it.

That matters because the biggest problems—healthcare, infrastructure, hiring, education—rarely yield to incremental thinking alone. Young’s view is that transformation requires a jolt: a willingness to question the framework, not just optimize within it. For leaders, that is a hard shift. It asks them to accept discomfort as part of progress.

Why AI helps, but does not solve the problem

The conversation turns sharply when AI comes up. Young does not dismiss it. In fact, he sees AI as powerful for combinatorial innovation, meaning the ability to discover new connections across large sets of information at scale. That can be especially useful in scientific discovery and other fields where hidden relationships matter.

But he also warns that AI is strongest around the known. If organizations use it mainly for efficiency, optimization, and cost reduction, they may simply produce more of the same. AI can accelerate the statistical center—the most probable answer—but it does not naturally push teams toward the unknown, the messy, or the unconventional.

That distinction matters for executives. AI can help companies move faster, but speed alone does not create reinvention. Young’s point is that humans still need to introduce the “zig and zag” that takes ideas outside the obvious path. Without that human intervention, AI may amplify the existing system instead of transforming it.

From one-off innovation to an innovation marketplace

Young’s solution is built around a few linked ideas: an “innovation genome,” a “possibility commons,” and a broader innovation marketplace. In his model, an idea is not treated as a one-off asset that lives or dies inside a single team. Instead, it is connected horizontally to related ideas, adjacent industries, and larger moonshots—big goals such as rebuilding education or tackling a community challenge.

That structure is meant to help organizations see more than isolated inventions. It creates a path for ideas to be modular, interoperable, and stackable over time. In other words, one breakthrough can support the next rather than disappearing into an innovation graveyard. Young believes this would reduce waste and help organizations recirculate value they currently abandon.

He also imagines a marketplace where originators keep credit while others contribute to development and eventual deployment. That traceability matters, especially in a world where collaboration spans corporations, nonprofits, universities, and independent builders. It is a model that acknowledges a hard truth: the person who invents something is not always the person best equipped to commercialize it.

For leaders, the practical takeaway is clear. Innovation should not be treated as a closed, internal pipeline. It should be designed as a system that can move ideas across boundaries, preserve attribution, and make it easier for good work to find a second life.

Terry Young’s full conversation offers a thoughtful challenge to how organizations think about innovation, AI, and transformation. If you are rethinking how ideas move through your enterprise, it is worth a listen.
