2026-8-31 | AI at Work: Skills, Security, Robots | Embracing Digital This Week

AI is moving from experimental pilots into day-to-day operations, while cybersecurity, workforce skills, and robotics are becoming the central issues leaders must manage at the same time.

Artificial intelligence is no longer confined to labs or isolated pilots. Across business, security, education, and robotics, the technology is moving into real workflows and public life, forcing leaders to confront a new set of practical questions: how to govern AI use, how to defend against AI-amplified threats, and how to prepare people and systems for faster change. The central message is not that AI is arriving someday, but that it is already reshaping operations and risk management now.

AI Is Moving Closer to Core Operations

One of the clearest developments is the rise of forward-deployed AI, where companies are pushing models into real workflows rather than treating them as side projects. That shift matters because it changes AI from a concept demo into an operational tool that must deliver usable output, fit business processes, and be managed at scale.

At the same time, AI tools are reaching a broader audience. Access is expanding through national programs and through everyday creative work, which suggests demand is spreading beyond specialized technical teams. The practical implication for executives is straightforward: broad access without governance can create inconsistency, but broad access with training and clear controls can unlock more value.

Why Governance and Training Now Matter More

As AI becomes more widely available, organizations need to pay more attention to how people use it and what standards guide that use. The challenge is not only whether the tool works, but whether the output is reliable, usable, and appropriate for the task at hand. That makes governance and training central enablers, not optional extras.

Leaders should treat AI adoption as an operating-model change. If employees are expected to use AI in daily work, then policies, oversight, and skills development need to scale with that expectation. Without that foundation, companies risk inconsistent results and weak value capture even when the technology itself is capable.

Cybersecurity Pressure Is Rising Alongside AI

AI is also amplifying cybersecurity concerns at a time when major breaches continue to surface. That combination is important: the same broad access and automation that can improve productivity can also help attackers move faster and adapt more quickly.

Ransomware groups are already adopting newer development tools, which gives them speed and flexibility. In plain terms, attackers are not standing still. They are using better tooling to make their operations more efficient, which raises the stakes for defenders. For organizations, this means security teams need to assume that adversaries will increasingly use AI-enabled methods to scale attacks, refine payloads, and respond faster to defenses.

The lesson for executives is to connect AI strategy with security strategy. Expanding AI use inside the enterprise without strengthening protection for data, identities, and systems increases exposure. The more AI touches business-critical information, the more important it becomes to control what data is accessible, who can use it, and how it is monitored.

Building the Cyber Skills Pipeline

The response to growing cyber risk cannot come from enterprises alone. Fortinet’s free cybersecurity curriculum for U.S. high schools points to a wider effort to address the talent gap earlier in the education pipeline. That matters because the cybersecurity skills shortage is not just an enterprise staffing issue; it is a long-term workforce issue.

This kind of program suggests a more proactive model: rather than waiting until workers enter the job market, organizations and education providers are starting to build foundational cyber awareness sooner. For business leaders, the practical takeaway is that workforce resilience depends on more than hiring. It also depends on partnerships, training pathways, and investments that expand the future supply of security talent.

Robots Are Becoming Part of the Enterprise Fabric

Robotics is also moving deeper into the enterprise. The trend is no longer just about automation in isolated settings; it is increasingly about robots becoming part of core operations. That shift signals that firms are looking beyond efficiency gains and toward systems that can work alongside existing processes more directly.

There is also evidence of a move toward shared workflows rather than automation alone. Human-robot collaboration changes the design problem: organizations must think about how people and machines coordinate, how responsibilities are divided, and how work is supervised. This is especially relevant for leaders evaluating robotics investments, because the value comes not just from replacing tasks, but from redesigning work in a way that makes human and machine capabilities complementary.

Market signals from China reinforce the point that demand for AI and robotics is accelerating. For global executives, that suggests the competitive landscape is tightening. If major markets are moving quickly, firms elsewhere may need to accelerate their own experimentation and capability-building to avoid falling behind.

What Leaders Should Do Next

The combined signal across AI, security, and robotics is that digital transformation is becoming more integrated and more consequential. Leaders should focus on three priorities: strengthen governance, invest in workforce readiness, and align technology adoption with security requirements. These are not separate initiatives; they are linked parts of the same transition.

In practical terms, that means setting clear rules for AI use, training employees to use tools effectively, and ensuring security teams have the visibility and controls needed to defend against faster-moving threats. It also means thinking beyond pilots and asking how AI and robotics will change everyday operations, not just isolated functions.

What leaders can take forward

AI is now influencing how organizations work, how attackers operate, and how the next generation of skills is developed. The companies that benefit most will be the ones that treat adoption, security, and workforce planning as one connected agenda rather than three separate problems.
